## Introduction

You cannot make a system faster until you know what limits it. In this
assignment you will take one training step of a large language model apart
on a single GPU, find out where its time and its memory go, and learn the
tools for finding that out: GPU timers, the GPU's own counters, and
NVIDIA's profiler, Nsight Systems.

A GPU offers a program three resources:

- **compute**: arithmetic operations per second (TFLOP/s);
- **memory bandwidth**: bytes per second moved between memory and the cores (GB/s);
- **memory capacity**: bytes that fit at once (GiB).

Throughout this assignment the question is the same: which resource is the
limit here?

The model is [OLMoE-1B-7B](https://huggingface.co/allenai/OLMoE-1B-7B-0924),
a mixture-of-experts (MoE) language model from the Allen Institute for AI.
It has 6.9 billion parameters, but any one token uses only 1.3 billion of
them. That gap shapes almost everything about how the model performs, and
you will meet it in all three assignments of this course:

| Assignment | You will | GPUs |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Measure where time and memory go in a training step | 1 |
| 2 | Add data and expert parallelism, fine-tune the whole model, and compete on throughput | 1–8 |
| 3 | Serve your fine-tuned model with vLLM and SGLang, and study inference configurations | 1–8 |

You will not write any model code. The file `olmoe/model.py` is a complete
and reasonably efficient OLMoE. Your job is to build a few small measuring
tools, to measure, and to explain what you measure.

The assignment has four parts:

1. **One operation.** You learn to time GPU code correctly, to bound how
   fast an operation can run with the roofline model, and to read the GPU's
   own counters.
2. **One forward pass.** You examine a forward pass of the real model with
   Nsight Systems.
3. **One training step.** You take a step apart: its forward pass, its
   backward pass and its optimizer, the memory they need, and two ways of
   trading time for memory, activation recomputation and micro batches.
4. **Predicting performance.** You write a small model that predicts the
   time and memory of training steps you have never run.

## Part 1: One Operation

Before profiling a model, you need to be able to measure one operation and
to say what limits it. The operation in this part is the one that language
models spend nearly all of their arithmetic on: a matrix multiply.

### Timing GPU Code

When Python calls `torch.matmul(a, b)` on GPU tensors, the call returns
almost at once. It has only *enqueued* a kernel, and the GPU runs the
kernel later. Timing the call with a clock on the CPU therefore measures
the time to enqueue, not the time to multiply. Here are three ways of
timing the same multiply of two 4096 × 4096 matrices.

The first reads the CPU's clock before and after the call:

```python
t0 = time.time()
torch.matmul(a, b)
t1 = time.time()
```

The second waits for the GPU to finish before it reads the clock again:

```python
t0 = time.time()
torch.matmul(a, b)
torch.cuda.synchronize()
t1 = time.time()
```

The third does not use the CPU's clock at all. It puts a *CUDA event*
before the multiply and another after it, and asks for the time between
the two:

```python
start = torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True)
end = torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True)
start.record()
torch.matmul(a, b)
end.record()
torch.cuda.synchronize()
ms = start.elapsed_time(end)
```

On an L40, after a warm-up that we come back to in a moment, the three
report:

```text
1: no synchronize       0.021 ms
2: with synchronize     0.841 ms
3: CUDA events          0.847 ms
```

The first number is wrong by a factor of forty: it is the time Python took
to hand the work to the GPU. The second is right, because
`torch.cuda.synchronize()` blocks until the GPU has finished everything it
was given. The third is right too, and it is the one we will use. The two
differ by six microseconds, less than one percent, although each timed its
own multiply. A *CUDA event* is a marker placed in the GPU's queue. The
GPU stamps it with the time at which it reaches it, so the time between
two events is the time the GPU spent on the work between them, and nobody
had to wait in the middle. That matters once you want to time the parts of
something larger without disturbing it, as you will in Part 3.

Those numbers were taken after a *warm-up*: the multiply had already run
for a tenth of a second, untimed. The first calls of anything on a GPU pay
for things that later calls do not. Libraries are loaded. Memory is
requested from the driver, which takes a third of a millisecond or more
for a block of 128 MiB. PyTorch then keeps the block in a cache of its
own, and hands it out again in a few microseconds. A training run makes
millions of calls, and nearly all of them find their memory in that cache.
So the
time worth knowing is the *steady-state* time: what every call pays, and
nothing that only the first calls pay. Measuring it after a warm-up is
standard practice, and every time in this assignment is measured that
way. The scripts call `warm_up`, in `olmoe/prof.py`, before they measure
anything: it runs a function untimed at least twice, and for at least a
tenth of a second.

The scripts follow two more rules, so that a time means the same thing
wherever it appears. When a script times a function, it times at least
five calls and reports the median, so that one disturbed call does not
matter. (There are two exceptions. `regimes.py`, in Part 1, reports the
average over four seconds of calls, and a recording for Nsight, in Part 2,
holds only two passes, to stay small.) And every training step that is
timed takes at least 100 ms, which for our model means a few thousand
tokens. Shorter steps can be timed, but their times are not worth much:
most of such a step does not depend on its tokens at all, and that part
moves by several percent from one run to the next, whatever the warm-up.

A warm-up does not make every later call equally fast, though. As you are
about to find out, a GPU that has just been idle runs faster than one that
has been busy for a second, and one that has been busy for a few minutes
is slower still.

### The Roofline

Every kernel does arithmetic, and it moves bytes between memory and the
cores. An L40 can do at most **181 TFLOP/s** of bf16 matrix arithmetic, and
it can move at most **864 GB/s**. So a kernel that performs `F`
floating-point operations and must move `B` bytes takes at least
`F / 181e12` seconds, and also at least `B / 864e9` seconds. Whichever is
larger is its bound:

- if the first is larger, the kernel is **compute-bound**;
- if the second is larger, it is **memory-bound**.

The *roofline* puts both bounds on one plot. A kernel's *arithmetic
intensity* is `F / B`, in FLOPs per byte, and the most TFLOP/s it can reach
is

```text
min(peak compute, intensity × peak bandwidth)
```

The two meet at the *ridge point*, 181e12 / 864e9 ≈ 210 FLOPs per byte.
Below it a kernel is memory-bound, and above it compute-bound.

Take the matrix multiply `C = torch.matmul(A, B)` in bf16, two bytes per
number, where `A` is `[M, k]`, `B` is `[k, n]`, and `C` is `[M, n]`. It
performs `M * n * k` multiply-adds, which is `2 * M * n * k`
FLOPs. It must at least read `A` and `B` and write `C`:
`2 * (M * k + k * n + M * n)` bytes. As an example, take one of the
attention projections of our model, a 2048 × 2048 weight matrix, applied to
`M = 16` tokens:

```text
FLOPs      = 2 * 16 * 2048 * 2048                        = 134 million
bytes      = 2 * (16 * 2048 + 2048 * 2048 + 16 * 2048)   = 8.5 million
intensity  = 134e6 / 8.5e6                               = 15.8 FLOPs per byte
```

That is far below the ridge point, so this multiply is memory-bound: its 8
megabytes of weights have to be read however few tokens there are. The best
it can do is 8.5e6 / 864e9 = 10 microseconds, at 15.8 × 864 GB/s = 13.6
TFLOP/s, a thirteenth of the GPU's peak. Give the same matrix 4096 tokens
and the intensity is 819: now it is compute-bound, and could run at the
full 181 TFLOP/s.

Notice what happened. `M` is a number of tokens and `B` is a weight matrix.
With few tokens a layer is limited by the bytes of its weights, and with
many it is limited by its arithmetic. This one observation will explain a
great deal of what follows.

### Three Regimes

There is a third regime, which the roofline does not show. Launching a
kernel has a fixed cost of some microseconds, on the CPU and on the GPU.
When the work is small enough, that cost is all there is: the kernel is
**overhead-bound**, and neither resource is anywhere near its limit.

You can tell the three regimes apart without a model, because the GPU
counts what it does. `a1/regimes.py` runs one workload of each kind for a
few seconds, and samples the GPU's own counters while it runs:

```sh
(cos568) $ python a1/regimes.py
large GEMM, 8192^3            11.2834 ms  kernel running  96%  memory busy  15%  1383 MHz  265 W
add two 1 GiB tensors          4.9464 ms  kernel running  98%  memory busy  98%  2490 MHz  212 W
small GEMM, 64 x 2048 x 1024   0.0150 ms  kernel running  51%  memory busy   0%  2490 MHz  146 W
```

The first number on each line is the time of one call. From this output
you can conclude a few things:

- The large GEMM is compute-bound. A kernel is running nearly all of the
  time, but memory is being read or written only 15% of the time: the
  arithmetic works out of the caches.
- Adding two large tensors is memory-bound. There is almost no arithmetic
  to do, and memory is busy 98% of the time.
- The small GEMM is overhead-bound. Half of the time no kernel is running
  at all, although the loop launches them as fast as Python can. The GPU is
  waiting for the CPU.

The counters come from NVML, the library behind `nvidia-smi`, through the
`GpuMonitor` class in `olmoe/prof.py`. They average over up to a second, so
they are only meaningful for a workload that runs steadily for a few
seconds. Look at the last two columns as well, the clock frequency of the
GPU's cores and its power draw. They are not the same on every line.

### The Model in Numbers

Each of the 16 layers has an attention block, with four 2048 × 2048
projections, and an MoE block with 64 experts. An expert is three matrices:
`gate_proj` and `up_proj`, from 2048 to 1024 dimensions, and `down_proj`,
from 1024 back to 2048. A router sends every token to its 8 best experts
and adds up their outputs.

| Part of the model | Parameters |
|---|---|
| One layer: attention, 4 × 2048 × 2048 | 16.8 million |
| One layer: 64 experts, each 3 × 2048 × 1024 | 402.7 million |
| One layer: router and norms | 0.1 million |
| 16 layers | 6.71 billion |

### Nsight Systems

Nsight Systems (`nsys`) records every kernel the GPU runs and every CUDA
call the CPU makes, on one timeline. To find your own code in that
timeline, you mark regions of it with *NVTX ranges*:
`torch.cuda.nvtx.range_push("name")` opens one and `range_pop()` closes it.
The model already marks two regions in each MoE layer, `router` and
`experts`, and `train_step` marks `forward`, `backward` and `optimizer`.

One thing about ranges is easy to get wrong. A range marks a stretch of
*CPU* time, the time during which your code *launched* kernels. The kernels
themselves run later. Nsight can *project* a range onto the GPU, which
means taking the span of the kernels that were launched from inside it.
That is the number you usually want.

## Part 3: One Training Step

A training step has three phases. The **forward** pass computes the loss,
and keeps the activations that the backward pass will need. The
**backward** pass computes a gradient for every weight. The **optimizer**,
AdamW, updates every weight. See `train_step` in `olmoe/train.py`: it is
about ten lines.

In this assignment the weights, their gradients, and AdamW's two moments
are all stored in bf16, two bytes each. That is 8 bytes per parameter. For
all 16 layers it comes to 51.5 GiB, more than the L40's 44 GiB, before a
single activation is stored. A model that does not fit on the GPU can
still be trained there by *offloading*: part of that state is kept in the
host's memory, and moved to the GPU only while it is needed. Offloading is
a valid way to train large models, but we will not consider it in this
assignment. Instead, in this part we train only the first four layers
(`--layers 4`, 1.9 billion parameters). The layers are identical in shape,
so what you learn about four of them carries over. The experiments in
Parts 3 and 4 use random weights and random tokens, so that they need no
checkpoint.

### Recomputation

The forward pass keeps activations for the backward pass, and their size
grows with the number of tokens and with the number of layers. With enough
tokens, they are what fills the GPU's memory.

*Activation recomputation*, also called checkpointing, keeps only the input
of each layer. When the backward pass reaches a layer, it first runs that
layer's forward again, to rebuild the activations, and then computes the
layer's gradients as usual. Only one layer's activations exist at a time,
and the price is that every layer's forward runs twice in each step. It
trades compute for memory capacity.

A step with recomputation therefore has four kinds of work, and the rest of
this part accounts for each of them separately: **forward**, **backward**,
**recompute**, and **optimizer**.

### Micro Batches and Gradient Accumulation

Recomputation is one way to make a step fit in memory. Here is a second
one. It rests on a property of the loss.

Two different things are called "the batch" of a step, and from here on we
keep them apart. The **global batch** is the set of sequences that one
optimizer update is computed from, and the **global batch size** is how
many sequences that is. A **micro batch** is the set of sequences that goes
through the model in one forward and backward pass, and the **micro batch
size** is how many sequences that is. So far every step has run its global
batch as a single micro batch, so the two sizes were equal. They need not
be.

The loss of a step is the average, over all tokens of its global batch, of
each token's cross-entropy:

```text
loss  =  (l_1 + l_2 + ... + l_N) / N
```

Here `l_i` is the cross-entropy of token `i`, and `N` is the number of
*trainable* tokens in the global batch, the tokens that have a target.
Every one of them weighs `1/N`. The gradient of a sum is the sum of the
gradients, so the gradient that the optimizer needs is

```text
gradient  =  (gradient of l_1) / N  +  (gradient of l_2) / N  +  ...  +  (gradient of l_N) / N
```

That is a sum of `N` terms, and it does not matter in what groups they are
computed. So split the global batch into micro batches, each a few whole
sequences, and run the forward and the backward pass of one micro batch at
a time. Each backward pass produces the terms of its own tokens, and
PyTorch adds them to whatever is already in `.grad`. After the last micro
batch, `.grad` holds the gradient of the whole global batch, and the
optimizer steps once. This is **gradient accumulation**.

The argument needs two things, and you should be able to say why:

- A token's loss depends only on the tokens of its own sequence. Nothing in
  our model connects one sequence to another: attention never crosses a
  sequence boundary, the router looks at one token at a time, and there is
  no batch normalization. A micro batch therefore computes `l_i` for its
  tokens exactly as the whole global batch would in a single pass.
- Every token's loss is divided by `N`, the number of trainable tokens in
  the *global* batch, and not by the number in its own micro batch. This is
  why `lm_loss` in `olmoe/train.py` adds the cross-entropies up
  (`reduction="sum"`) and divides by a count that you pass in, instead of
  taking the usual mean. With the mean, a token in a micro batch of few
  tokens would weigh more than a token in a micro batch of many, and when
  sequences have different lengths, micro batches of the same size rarely
  have the same number of tokens.

The update is then the same whether a global batch of 16 sequences runs as
one micro batch of 16 or as sixteen micro batches of 1. It is the same *up
to rounding*: floating-point addition gives slightly different results when
the same numbers are added in a different order. The two gradients agree to
several digits, not bit for bit, and the tests compare them with a small
tolerance.

What does change is the cost. Only one micro batch's activations are alive
at a time, so the memory that a step needs depends on its micro batch size,
while the update depends only on its global batch. In exchange, the model
makes several small passes instead of one large one. The optimizer still
runs once per step.

One more observation, on which Assignment 2 is built. Nothing in the
argument says that the micro batches must run one after another, or on the
same GPU. Give each of several GPUs some of the global batch's sequences as
its micro batch, let each compute the terms of its own tokens with the same
`1/N`, and add the results up: the update is the same again. That is **data
parallelism**. Gradient accumulation spreads a global batch over time, data
parallelism spreads it over GPUs, and the two can be combined.

### Timing the Stages of a Step

How long does each layer take, in each of the four kinds of work? You can
find out with CUDA events and two kinds of hooks.

A *forward hook* is a function that you attach to a module. PyTorch calls
it every time the module's forward has finished, and passes it the output.
A *tensor hook* is a function that you attach to a tensor. PyTorch calls it
during the backward pass, when the gradient of that tensor has been
computed.

Here is a small example that uses both. Its model has two stages, the
linear layers `first` and `second`, and `x` goes through one and then the
other. Each stage gets a forward hook, and that hook attaches a tensor hook
to the stage's output:

```python
first = torch.nn.Linear(8, 8)     # a model with two stages:
second = torch.nn.Linear(8, 8)    #     x -> first -> second -> loss
x = torch.randn(4, 8)

def watch(name, module):
    def forward_hook(module, args, output):   # called when the forward of `module` has finished
        print(f"forward:  {name} has finished")
        output.register_hook(tensor_hook)

    def tensor_hook(grad):                    # called when the gradient of `output` is known
        print(f"backward: about to enter {name}")

    module.register_forward_hook(forward_hook)

watch("first", first)
watch("second", second)

loss = second(first(x)).sum()
print("calling loss.backward()")
loss.backward()
```

This prints

```text
forward:  first has finished
forward:  second has finished
calling loss.backward()
backward: about to enter second
backward: about to enter first
```

The forward hooks fire in the order of the stages. The tensor hooks fire in
the reverse order, during `loss.backward()`: the backward pass goes through
the stages from last to first, and the gradient of a stage's output becomes
known at exactly the moment the backward pass is about to enter that stage.
So the hooks give you the boundaries
between the stages in both directions, and a CUDA event recorded at each
boundary gives you their times.

Recomputation needs one more boundary. A recomputed forward is an ordinary
forward of the layer, so its hooks fire again, but in the middle of the
backward pass. `olmoe/prof.py` has a function for telling the two apart:
`in_backward()` is true while a backward pass is running. A forward
*pre*-hook, which PyTorch calls when a module's forward is about to start,
and a forward hook then give you the start and the end of the
recomputation. Register the forward hook with `always_call=True`, as in
Coding Exercise 4: PyTorch stops a recomputed forward as soon as it has rebuilt
what it needs, by raising an exception.

## Part 4: Predicting Performance

A performance model is the difference between having measured a system and
understanding it. If you can predict the time and memory of a configuration
you have never run, you know where the costs come from.

### A Sweep of Your Own

`a1/sweep.py` measures training steps over a grid of configurations. For
each one it records the step time, the four kinds of work in it, throughput, MFU
(model FLOPs utilization, the share of peak compute spent on the model's
arithmetic), peak memory, and the GPU's counters:

```sh
(cos568) $ python a1/sweep.py --micro-batch-sizes 2 8 --seqlens 1024
(heated the chip to 60 C first: 35 s)
 4 layers   2 x 1024   111.5 ms  18,366 tok/s  MFU 23.2%  14.2 GiB  kernel  97%  memory  81%  2415 MHz  58 C
 4 layers   8 x 1024   291.3 ms  28,122 tok/s  MFU 35.4%  22.5 GiB  kernel  99%  memory  70%  2096 MHz  58 C
appended to results/a1/sweep.csv
```

The last four columns are the counters of Part 1: the share of time a
kernel was running, the share of time memory was busy, the clock, and the
temperature of the chip when the step had been timed. Rows
accumulate in `results/a1/sweep.csv` from one run to the next, so you can
build up a table over several runs. Delete the file to start over.

Run the same line twice and the times will differ. How much is worth
knowing before you fit a model to them. Measured again straight away, a
step repeats within 1 or 2%. Another node, or the same node on another
day, can differ by 3 or 4%. The largest difference would come from the
last column. A chip that has been
working for a few minutes is 20 to 30 degrees hotter than one that has
just started. It reaches its power limit at a lower clock, and the
compute-bound part of a step slows down with the clock: a step of
4 × 2048 takes 6 to 9% longer. The part that is bound by memory bandwidth
does not change.

That is what the first line of the output above is about. `sweep.py` does
not measure on a cold chip. Before every configuration it looks at the
temperature, and below 60 °C it first keeps the GPU busy with a large GEMM
until the chip gets there (`heat_up`, in `olmoe/prof.py`). From an idle
GPU that takes about 40 seconds. Once a sweep is under way it takes none.
The rows of your table then all come from a chip above 55 °C, and the
first rows of a sweep are 1 or 2% faster than they would be later on, not
3 to 7%. The other scripts of this assignment do not do this: their times
come from the chip as they find it, and `stages.py` prints the temperature
so that you can tell.

A step shorter than 100 ms is another matter. `sweep.py` still measures
it, and marks it with a star:

```sh
(cos568) $ python a1/sweep.py --layers 2 --micro-batch-sizes 1 8 --seqlens 1024
(heated the chip to 60 C first: 3 s)
 2 layers   1 x 1024    53.9 ms  18,998 tok/s  MFU 15.2%   7.8 GiB  kernel  90%  memory  79%  2490 MHz  56 C *
 2 layers   8 x 1024   179.1 ms  45,737 tok/s  MFU 36.6%  14.2 GiB  kernel  99%  memory  68%  2032 MHz  60 C
* shorter than 100 ms: too short a step to time reliably. Give it more tokens.
appended to results/a1/sweep.csv
```

Leave such steps out of your sweep. None of the configurations that your
predictor is graded on is that short.
